**Abstract:**

The number of retracted scientific publications has risen dramatically over recent years, prompting questions about the underlying causes of this trend. It is unclear whether this increase is due to flawed articles being published or improvements in the detection and removal of problematic papers. 
To explore this issue, we analyzed 2,047 retracted articles indexed in PubMed. The average time-to-retraction across all articles was 32.91 months. However, articles published before 2002 took significantly longer to retract (49.82 months) compared to those published after 2002 (23.82 months, p < 0.0001). 
We examined the relationship between time-to-retraction and journal impact factor (IF) to assess the role of increased scrutiny. While high-IF journals tended to retract articles more quickly, this factor explained only 1% of retraction time variance. The correlation was stronger for articles retracted due to misconduct, 
especially fraud, but still modest overall. Additionally, the study found that retractions are more likely to occur earlier in the article’s life cycle, with a progressive decline in time-to-retraction for articles published since 2000. From 1993 to 2012, 63.1% of retracted papers were written by authors with only one retraction,
compared to 46% from 1972 to 1992 (p < 0.001). In contrast, authors with multiple retractions had a greater influence in the earlier period but contributed less proportionally to the recent surge in retractions. 
In conclusion, the rise in retractions reflects changes in the behavior of both authors and institutions. These findings underscore the importance of continued vigilance in maintaining the integrity of the scientific record.
